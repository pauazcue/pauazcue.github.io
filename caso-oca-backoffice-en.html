Commercial Backoffice

We were asked to review the UI of a new billing system. What we found was much more than a design problem: an entire process built around the limitations of the old system, not around the people doing the billing.

Role
Senior Product Designer · Design Lead
Team
2 people (me + 1 junior)
Scope
Rating · Billing · Auditing
Industry
Logistics · Internal billing
Flows and screens · image coming soon
Context

OCA moves more than two million parcels a month. And it used to bill all of them at once, at month's end.

With more than 125 branches and 1,000 pickup and delivery points across the country, OCA is Argentina's largest logistics company. That whole operation generated billing every month — and the system running it was COBOL: decades of accumulated technical debt, complex, with no consideration for the user experience.

The billing team — five or six people — couldn't work from home, had to come in on Saturdays when the monthly close hit, and found it practically impossible to take vacation during billing season. It wasn't a limitation of the team. It was a limitation of the system.

The problem wasn't only technological. The entire process had been designed around the old system's limitations, not around the people doing the billing.

The problem

They closed the month on a Saturday. Billing errors were frequent. And no one could take vacation.

The billing process was a human bottleneck: the entire shipment calculation happened at month's end, all at once. If something failed — and it did — the team absorbed it with overtime.

Billing errors were frequent. OCA's commercial culture prioritized retaining clients at any cost — even clients that weren't profitable. To accommodate demands, the commercial team manually adjusted numbers, generating a high volume of credit and debit notes that delayed collections: a bounced invoice required issuing a credit note, redoing the invoice and resending it.

The diagnosis

We were asked to review the UI. We found there was no UX.

The agency had delivered screens. Nothing else. There were no user flows, no navigation logic, no consideration of intermediate states, errors or validations. They were loose screens that replicated the COBOL interface in new technology — the tech changed, but not the problem.

I raised it with the PM: this wasn't a UI problem. It was a product problem. It got validated, and we had to solve it ourselves.

The process

Discovery, flows and a junior designer to mentor.

01

Discovery with the billing team

We interviewed the team to understand how they worked, what steps they took, where they got stuck, and what information they needed to see and when. The goal was documenting the real process before designing the new system.

02

Defining flows from scratch

We mapped every flow the system needed to support: bulk billing, individual billing with client-specific specs, rate updates, subscription and advance management, change auditing. Every flow validated with operations and the technical team before moving to screens.

03

Guidelines and handoff to the agency

The agency remained responsible for high-fidelity UI. I defined the flows at low fidelity, handed over Ñandú's guidelines (the design system under construction), and acted as UAT on the design side to make sure the implementation didn't lose what we had defined.

04

Mentoring the junior designer

With someone with little experience on the project, I guided her toward research and information gathering, teaching her to think through flows and build low-fidelity screens before moving to high fidelity.

The impact

A system that changes how OCA bills — and how the team works.

The system is in development. Once it launches:

→

Real-time billing

Each shipment's rate is calculated on the spot. No more waiting for month's end — the team can run closes on any date without depending on rigid closing windows.

→

Control and auditing

Business rules live in the system, not in each account manager's head. This shrinks the room for manual adjustments that used to generate credit notes out of error or pressure.

→

A team that can work differently

No closes tying them to the office on Saturdays. With visibility and control instead of having to manually solve what the software should be solving for them.
