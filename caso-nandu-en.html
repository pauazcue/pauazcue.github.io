Ñandú

The shared language to scale OCA's digital ecosystem

OCA had multiple products running simultaneously, each making its own design decisions. Ñandú is the shared infrastructure — tokens, components and criteria — I built to unify them without slowing teams down.

The starting point

Components weren't missing. A shared language was.

When I joined OCA, there was no shared design tool like Figma, nor a Design System to unify criteria. ePak, OMS, Backoffice and other platforms had been built at different times, by different teams, each making its own decisions.

The differences went beyond the interface: components, typography, colors, iconography, interaction patterns, even how actions and messages were communicated to users. Every new project solved problems other teams had already faced.

I started the project as a Product Designer. As a team we knew we needed a Design System, but I was the one who pushed to prioritize it, name it and give it structure — and I ended up leading its construction.

The challenge

The goal wasn't to build a component library.

We needed to create a system that helped make design decisions consistently, made collaboration between Design and Development easier, and supported the growth of OCA's digital ecosystem.

The challenge was finding the balance between standardizing what was necessary and keeping enough flexibility for each product to evolve on its own terms.

Building the design system while the products were already in production meant a constant balancing act: moving forward without breaking what already worked, with a team that had other active priorities.

An identity of its own

The Design System also needed its own identity.

Together with the Design team, we defined the name, personality and visual identity of the system until we landed on Ñandú.

We chose the name for what it represents: the ñandú is a bird native to our land, fast and in constant motion — an image that connects directly with the idea of a living, evolving system. The letter Ñ also reinforces OCA's Argentine, national character — a company present across the whole country.

I worked on this process together with Branding: we defined name, colors, typography and graphic resources that later started being used in institutional and communication pieces too, strengthening a consistent visual identity across the organization.

The foundations of the system

From that identity we built the system's infrastructure.

We defined foundations like color, typography, spacing, iconography and composition rules, which led to 33 documented interface components — each with its own states and variants, not just a generic library of buttons and inputs.

Every decision aimed to reduce repetitive work and let teams spend their time solving product problems, instead of redesigning the same elements over and over.

Built to evolve

Ñandú wasn't designed as a closed library.

Every new product helped validate existing components, surface new patterns or bring improvements into the system.

ePak was the first product to adopt the system fully, and afterward OMS, Backoffice and other projects started reusing its components and patterns, building a common language across teams. Today 4 digital products at OCA — ePak, OMS, Commercial Backoffice and Communications — run on the same tokens and base components.

The Design System evolved alongside the products. The products also strengthened the Design System.

Documentation and adoption

Building components mattered as much as making them easy to adopt.

We documented components, patterns, usage rules and best practices so any designer or developer could understand when to use each solution and keep consistency across products.

Ñandú stopped being a Figma library and became a shared source of truth across Design, Development and Product — today, 40% of the library is still under active expansion: a stable, already-consolidated core coexisting with parts of the system that keep evolving alongside each new product's needs.

Evolution with AI

I also structured Ñandú in Claude Design.

As part of the system's evolution, I structured Ñandú in Claude Design, which makes it possible to look up components, patterns and rules through natural language.

This representation makes it easier to keep the system up to date, share knowledge across teams and explore new ways of integrating AI assistants into the design and development process.

More than a copy of the Design System, Claude Design works as a knowledge layer on top of Ñandú, built for AI-assisted workflows.

Learnings

Building a Design System isn't about designing components.

It's about building a system of decisions that lets products evolve consistently, even as teams, priorities and business needs change.

Working on Ñandú taught me that a Design System creates value when it connects people, process and technology — and when it becomes a tool that helps the whole organization design better.
